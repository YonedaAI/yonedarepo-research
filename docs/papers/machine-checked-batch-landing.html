1 Introduction

Source code and source control are now transactional abstractions. A repository is a database, a change is a transaction that reads part of the source and writes another part, landing a change is a commit under optimistic concurrency control, and a version is a snapshot. Part I of this series gave the transaction model behind that view and proved its properties by hand [10]. Part II treated derived data as views maintained from the log [11], and Part III realized the model on cloud-portable primitives and measured it [12]. This paper makes the core of Part I’s model machine-checked. The definitions and proofs form a Lean 4 development [6], and we present its statements here, together with the counterexamples that show where each hypothesis is needed and the corrections that the formalization forced on Part I.

We argue that code is taking a step that data took first. Data lakes began as files on object stores; when concurrent writers and derived computation broke file-level semantics, the field moved to tables with a transaction log, snapshots and optimistic concurrency [3]. Agents put source control under the same pressure by multiplying concurrent writers. Scion’s published design responds in the same way, with server-side changesets recorded in a write-ahead log and a ref update by compare-and-swap on one database row [2]. A transactional foundation is useful only if its guarantees hold, and the guarantees that carry the most weight under many concurrent writers are those about batched landing.

Batching is also where hand proofs are most exposed. A combiner that lands many changes in one call to the serialized authority validates each member against the committed log, but not against the other members. Part I showed that this admits write skew (Part I, Theorem 7.3) and that a pairwise rule removes it for members validated as fast-forwards or rebases (Part I, Proposition 7.4). Members validated as Merge reach the head through a three-way merge, and Part I left them outside that proposition with only a description of the merged state (Part I, Proposition 6.5). The synthesis paper then uses the pairwise rule to treat batch size as a capacity parameter with no cost in isolation [13]. That conclusion depends on the rule being correct, and its reach depends on whether the rule extends to merged members.

The headline theorem settles both questions in the model. Take a log in which no landing was a Conflict, and a batch accepted at its head: every member validated as FastForward, as Rebase, or as a Merge whose three-way merge with the head has no conflict path, and no member reads or writes a location that an earlier member wrote. Suppose every Merge member is patch-compatible with the head, which means that on its write set, running its transaction on the head gives its own diff applied to the head. Then the batch installs exactly the serial execution of its members in ticket order, and the serialization graph over declared reads and writes is acyclic (Theorem 5.1). Neither extra hypothesis can be dropped. Without the pairwise rule, the write-skew batch is accepted and equals neither serial order (Proposition 5.3). Patch compatibility is the exact condition for one merged member (Theorem 4.3), and a blind write that never read the path it merged already violates it (Proposition 4.5).

The development proves the results the headline rests on as well. These are the frame lemma, serializability of logs whose landings are fast-forwards and rebases, the order-insensitivity of independent changes, and acyclicity of the serialization graph. It proves the laws of the three-way merge: its specification, uniqueness, commutativity, identities and idempotence. It shows that a file-level conflict between edits to disjoint lines becomes a clean merge when lines are modelled as child locations with stable keys (Theorem 4.2). And it asks what survives when writers read more than they declare. No landed update is lost, serializability can fail, and a sufficient condition for serializability cannot be weakened in general (Section 6). Concrete instances show that every main hypothesis can hold nontrivially (Section 7).

Formalizing changed four statements. The merge rule as Part I describes it makes two identical changes conflict, so idempotence fails. The frame lemma holds only because agreement and the write frame use different regions of the location tree. A merged landing is a serial step exactly under patch compatibility, and the two conditions one would guess first are not enough. And the premise that undeclared reads violate is coverage of true reads by declared ones, not Part I’s sound-refinement assumption. Section 8 states each as a correction to Part I.

Every result is about the model. The implementation, YonedaRepo, is first-party work by the author, and it is linked to the model only by the code-evidence appendices of Parts I and III, which rest on reading code at named commits. We therefore call the theorems machine-checked and do not call the implementation verified. The Lean sources are public in the directory lean/ of the series repository [14], and Appendix A maps every listing in this paper to a file and line.

The paper makes five contributions.

  1. A Lean 4 formalization of the concurrency core of Part I: paths and overlap, tree states and agreement, transactions with a frame condition, the log and its snapshots, validation, and file-level and line-level three-way merge (Section 2).

  2. A machine-checked batch landing theorem that covers members validated as Merge, under a hypothesis shown to be exact, with counterexamples for each hypothesis (Sections 4 and 5).

  3. Machine-checked proofs of Part I’s frame lemma, serializability theorem, merged-writes proposition, independence theorem and coarsening proposition, in the adapted forms stated in Section 3.

  4. Results on under-declared reads: no lost update, a counterexample to serializability, a sufficient condition and its tightness (Section 6).

  5. Four corrections to Part I found by formalizing (Section 8), and a statement of what the formalization leaves out (Section 9).

2 The formal model

The development uses core Lean 4 at version 4.35.0, with no library beyond the standard prelude. Each definition below is stated in the notation of Part I and then shown as the Lean source it abbreviates. Listings quote statements and definitions. Proofs are omitted, including the proof fields of structure instances such as Txn.changeset, except where a short proof is an argument of a definition; the documentation comments of the source are dropped. Where the model departs from Part I we say so where the definition appears, and Section 9 collects the departures.

2.1 Paths and overlap

Definition 2.1 (Paths and overlap). A path is a finite sequence of string components. For paths \(a,b\), write \(a\sqsubseteq b\) when \(b=a\cdot t\) for some sequence \(t\). Two paths overlap, \(a\bowtie b\), when \(a\sqsubseteq b\) or \(b\sqsubseteq a\). An access set is a finite list of paths. For access sets \(X,Y\), \(X\bowtie Y\) holds when some \(x\in X\) and \(y\in Y\) overlap, and \(X\not\bowtie Y\) otherwise. The set covered by \(X\) is \(\mathrm{cov}(X)=\{p : x\sqsubseteq p \text{ for some } x\in X\}\), and the overlap region of \(X\) is \(\mathrm{reg}(X)=\{p : x\bowtie p \text{ for some } x\in X\}\).

This is Part I’s location tree (Part I, Definition 4.4) realized as paths. Directories, files and symbols are all nodes: a symbol entry such as src/lib.rs#Policy::backoff becomes the path with components src, lib.rs, Policy and backoff. Ancestry is component-wise prefix, so src/a is not an ancestor of src/ab, as in the implementation’s component-boundary test. The covered set \(\mathrm{cov}(X)\) is Part I’s \(\bigcup_{x\in X}\mathrm{sub}(x)\). The overlap region \(\mathrm{reg}(X)\) adds the proper ancestors of each element of \(X\).

Lemma 2.2 (Overlap; Part I, Lemma 4.5). \(a\bowtie b\) if and only if some path \(x\) satisfies \(a\sqsubseteq x\) and \(b\sqsubseteq x\). Overlap is reflexive and symmetric, and if \(a'\sqsubseteq a\) and \(a\bowtie b\) then \(a'\bowtie b\).

LSTBLOCK1X

Proof. If \(a\sqsubseteq b\), then \(b\) is a common descendant, and symmetrically. Conversely, two ancestors of one path are comparable: by induction on \(a\), two prefixes of the same sequence agree on their common length, so one extends the other. Reflexivity and symmetry are immediate from the definition. For the last claim, if \(a\sqsubseteq b\) then \(a'\sqsubseteq b\) by transitivity, and if \(b\sqsubseteq a\) then \(a'\) and \(b\) are both ancestors of \(a\) and hence comparable. ◻

2.2 States and agreement

Definition 2.3 (States, agreement and confinement). A state is a function \(s\) from paths to \(\mathbb{N}\cup\{\bot\}\), where \(\bot\) means absent and a natural number stands for a blob. Two states agree on an access set \(X\), written \(s\equiv_X t\), when \(s(p)=t(p)\) for every \(p\in\mathrm{reg}(X)\). A state \(t\) changes \(s\) only within \(W\) when \(t(p)=s(p)\) for every \(p\notin\mathrm{cov}(W)\).

Agreement in the model compares more locations than Part I’s relation, because every node of the tree carries its own content. Part I gives directories no content and compares the file and symbol locations in \(\bigcup_{x\in X}\mathrm{sub}(x)\) (Part I, Section 4.2). The Lean relation compares every path in \(\mathrm{reg}(X)\), including the proper ancestors of each \(x\), and the content of a node is independent of the content of its children. When the ancestors of the accessed locations carry no content, as directories do in Part I, the two relations coincide; this is an informal observation, not a Lean theorem. For an access below a file, such as a symbol or a line, the Lean relation also compares the file node, whose content the model does not derive from its children. The mappings of Part I’s results that conclude agreement are therefore adapted rather than literal.

Agreement and confinement use different regions on purpose: agreement inspects \(\mathrm{reg}(X)\), while a change is confined to the smaller set \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\). Section 8 shows that making the two regions equal breaks the frame lemma. Blobs are natural numbers so that concrete examples can be evaluated; any type with decidable equality would serve.

2.3 Transactions

Definition 2.4 (Transaction). A transaction \(c=(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{W},f)\) consists of access sets \(\mathsf{R}\) and \(\mathsf{W}\) and a function \(f\) from states to states such that

  1. (frame) \(f(s)(p)=s(p)\) for every state \(s\) and every \(p\notin\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\);

  2. (reads) if \(s\equiv_{\mathsf{R}\cup\mathsf{W}}t\), then \(f(s)(p)=f(t)(p)\) for every \(p\in\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\).

The frame condition is Part I’s sound-refinement assumption (Part I, Assumption 6.2) for one step at path granularity. The reads condition lets written values depend on \(\mathsf{W}\) as well as on \(\mathsf{R}\), which admits read-modify-write edits such as patching one line of a file. It is a weaker demand on transactions than dependence on \(\mathsf{R}\) alone, so every theorem below is at least as strong as its counterpart with that stricter condition. The set \(\mathsf{R}\) bounds dependence from above: nothing requires \(f\) to depend on every location of \(\mathsf{R}\).

LSTBLOCK3X

Definition 2.5 (Changeset). For access sets \(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{W}\) and a state \(\Phi\) of new content, the changeset \(\chi(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{W},\Phi)\) is the transaction with function \(s\mapsto s\triangleleft_{\mathsf{W}}\Phi\), where \((s\triangleleft_{\mathsf{W}}\Phi)(p)=\Phi(p)\) for \(p\in\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\) and \(s(p)\) otherwise.

This is Part I’s changeset (Part I, Definition 8.1) as a transaction. It is a blind write: its result on \(\mathsf{W}\) does not depend on the state it runs on, so the reads condition holds for any \(\mathsf{R}\).

2.4 The log and its snapshots

Definition 2.6 (Log and snapshots). A log entry \(e=(c,b)\) is a transaction and a base version. A log on one ref is a finite sequence \(\mathcal{L}=(e_1,\dots,e_n)\) with \(e_v=(c_v,b_v)\) and \(c_v=(\mathsf{R}_v,\mathsf{W}_v,f_v)\). Its snapshots from an initial state \(s_0\) are \[S_{0}=s_0,\qquad S_{v}=\mathrm{land}\bigl(S_{b'},\ S_{v-1},\ f_v(S_{b'})\bigr)\quad\text{with } b'=\min(b_v,v-1),\] where \(\mathrm{land}\) is the landing merge of Definition 2.8. The later write sets of a base \(b\) at head \(n\) are \(\mathrm{Later}(b,n)=(\mathsf{W}_v : b<v\leq n)\).

Version \(v\) is list position \(v-1\) in Lean. A range of versions \(b<u\leq v\) is therefore the range of positions \(b\leq i<v\), which is the form the Lean statements of the frame lemma and of later results use. The base is capped at the head, as the authority caps it at landing (Part I, Definition 4.16). Each change is computed on the snapshot at its own base, so Part I’s consistent-base assumption (Part I, Assumption 6.1) holds by construction, as it does for changesets (Part I, Proposition 8.3(2)). Commits and first parents do not appear in this log, and neither do refs other than the one it describes.

2.5 Validation

Definition 2.7 (Validation; Part I, Definition 4.15, adapted). For a list \(\ell\) of write sets and access sets \(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{W}\), validation returns \(\mathrm{val}(\ell,\mathsf{R},\mathsf{W})=\mathrm{Conflict}\) if \(\mathsf{R}\bowtie\mathsf{W}'\) for some \(\mathsf{W}'\) in \(\ell\); otherwise \(\mathrm{FastForward}\) if \(\ell\) is empty; otherwise \(\mathrm{Merge}\) if \(\mathsf{W}\bowtie\mathsf{W}'\) for some \(\mathsf{W}'\) in \(\ell\); and otherwise \(\mathrm{Rebase}\). Entry \(e_v\) is validated against \(\mathrm{Later}(b_v,v-1)\). A log has no Conflict when every entry satisfies \(b_v\leq v-1\) and its outcome is not \(\mathrm{Conflict}\); it is clean when every outcome is \(\mathrm{FastForward}\) or \(\mathrm{Rebase}\).

The definition is adapted. Part I’s stale-read test uses the later entries’ refined write sets \(\mathsf{W}^\rho\), its overlap test uses the unrefined ones, and it treats opaque entries as conflicts. Here one list of write sets serves both tests and no entry is opaque. Part I’s symbol-granularity corollary (Part I, Corollary 6.8), which depends on the two granularities, is therefore not formalized.

2.6 Three-way merge

Definition 2.8 (Three-way merge and landing). For values \(b,o,t\), let \(m_3(b,o,t)\) be \(t\) if \(o=b\); else \(o\) if \(t=b\); else \(o\) if \(o=t\); and a conflict otherwise. For states \(B,O,T\), the merge at \(p\) is \(m_3(B(p),O(p),T(p))\), and \(p\) is a conflict path when \(O(p)\neq B(p)\), \(T(p)\neq B(p)\) and \(O(p)\neq T(p)\). The merged state \(\mathrm{M}(B,O,T)\) takes the merged value at each path, with \(O(p)\) at a conflict path. The landing of a change \(C\) built on base \(B\) onto head \(H\) is \(\mathrm{land}(B,H,C)\): the merge with \(H\) as ours and \(C\) as theirs, with \(C(p)\) at a conflict path.

The third clause, under which two sides that made the same change do not conflict, is the rule of diff3 and Git; Section 8 shows what fails without it. The fallback at a conflict path keeps the log total. The two fallbacks, \(O(p)\) for \(\mathrm{M}\) and the change’s value for \(\mathrm{land}\), never affect a result: a conflict path differs from the base on the change’s side, so it lies in the change’s write set, and every theorem about \(\mathrm{M}\) assumes that no path is a conflict path. Part I’s authority refuses such landings, and no theorem below depends on the fallback beyond one property, that a landing changes the head only within the change’s write set: results about validated logs exclude conflict paths either by hypothesis or because a clean outcome has none.

Definition 2.9 (Diff). For states \(B,C,H\), the diff of \(C\) against \(B\) applied to \(H\) is \(\delta(B,C,H)(p)=C(p)\) if \(C(p)\neq B(p)\), and \(H(p)\) otherwise.

Lemma 2.10 (Merge as a diff). If no path is a conflict path of \((B,H,C)\), then \(\mathrm{M}(B,H,C)=\delta(B,C,H)\).

LSTBLOCK10X

Proof. At each path, split on whether \(H(p)=B(p)\), \(C(p)=B(p)\) and \(H(p)=C(p)\). If \(C(p)=B(p)\), both sides give \(H(p)\). If \(C(p)\neq B(p)\) and \(H(p)=B(p)\), both give \(C(p)\). If both differ from \(B(p)\), the absence of a conflict forces \(H(p)=C(p)\), and both give that value. ◻

2.7 Lines with stable keys

Definition 2.11 (Lines). For a file path \(f\) and a line key \(\ell\), the line \(\ell\) of \(f\) is the child location \(f\cdot\ell\), and the line map of \(f\) in \(s\) is \(\ell\mapsto s(f\cdot\ell)\). A file-granular conflict on \(f\) between \(O\) and \(T\) over \(B\) holds when the line map of \(f\) in \(O\) differs from that in \(B\), the map in \(T\) differs from that in \(B\), and the maps in \(O\) and \(T\) differ.

A file is thus a location whose children are its lines. Declared at file granularity, two edits to different lines of one file overlap; declared at line granularity, they do not. The keys are stable: inserting a line does not renumber the lines after it. Positional diff3, in which insertions and deletions shift line numbers and hunks must be aligned, is not modelled.

2.8 Batches

Definition 2.12 (Batch entry). A member \(m=(c,B)\) is a transaction and the state it was built on. Its merged change against a head \(H\) is \(\mu_H(m)=\mathrm{land}(B,H,f(B))\). The octopus merge of states \(M_1,\dots,M_k\) over \(H\) is \(\mathrm{oct}(H;\,)=H\) and \[\mathrm{oct}(H;M_1,\dots,M_k)(p)=\begin{cases}M_1(p) & \text{if } M_1(p)\neq H(p),\\ \mathrm{oct}(H;M_2,\dots,M_k)(p) & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\] The state installed by a batch \(m_1,\dots,m_k\) at head \(H\) is \(\mathrm{oct}(H;\mu_H(m_1),\dots,\mu_H(m_k))\).

This is the adapter’s octopus merge (Part I, Definition 7.1): each member is merged against the head, and at each path the first member whose merged change moved it wins. When the merged changes move disjoint paths, the order does not matter.

Definition 2.13 (Serial forms and the pairwise rule). For members \(m_1,\dots,m_k\) with \(m_i=(c_i,B_i)\) and a head \(H\), sequential landing folds \(s\mapsto\mathrm{land}(B_i,s,f_i(B_i))\) from \(H\); serial execution is \(f_k(\cdots f_1(H)\cdots)\); and the serial merge folds \(s\mapsto\mathrm{M}(B_i,s,f_i(B_i))\) from \(H\). The members satisfy the pairwise rule when \((\mathsf{R}_j\cup\mathsf{W}_j)\not\bowtie\mathsf{W}_i\) for all \(i<j\).

The pairwise rule is the condition of Part I, Proposition 7.4. A member built on a log entry \((c,b)\) is \((c,S_{b})\).

LSTBLOCK13X

Definition 2.14 (Patch compatibility). A member \(m=(c,B)\) is patch-compatible with a state \(H\), written \(\mathrm{PC}(m,H)\), when \(f(H)(p)=\delta(B,f(B),H)(p)\) for every \(p\in\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\).

Patch compatibility compares two readings of a change. The transactional reading runs \(c\) on the current head. The patch reading takes the diff that \(c\) made against its own base and applies it to the head. The condition says the readings agree on the write set; off the write set they agree for every transaction (Theorem 4.3).

Definition 2.15 (Accepted batch). Let \(\mathcal{L}\) have \(n\) entries and head \(H=S_{n}\). An entry \(e=(c,b)\) is accepted at \(H\) when \(b\leq n\) and \(\mathrm{val}(\mathrm{Later}(b,n),\mathsf{R},\mathsf{W})\) is \(\mathrm{FastForward}\) or \(\mathrm{Rebase}\), or is \(\mathrm{Merge}\) and no path is a conflict path of \((S_{b},H,f(S_{b}))\). A sequence of entries \(e_1,\dots,e_k\) is an accepted batch when every \(e_i\) is accepted and the pairwise rule holds in that order, which is ticket order.

Acceptance allows FastForward members, which Part I’s combiner lands alone; allowing them makes the theorems more general. A Merge member must merge without a conflict path at file granularity. Under the line model of Definition 2.11, that includes two edits to different lines of one file (Theorem 4.2).

2.9 Serialization graph

Definition 2.16 (Declared-access serialization graph). For a log, place the reads of entry \(v\) at version \(b_v\) and its writes at version \(v\). There is an edge \(u\to v\), for entries \(u\neq v\), when \(u<v\) and \(\mathsf{W}_u\bowtie\mathsf{W}_v\) (write-write); or \(u\leq b_v\) and \(\mathsf{W}_u\bowtie\mathsf{R}_v\) (write-read); or \(b_u<v\) and \(\mathsf{R}_u\bowtie\mathsf{W}_v\) (read-write).

This is the graph of the proof of Part I, Theorem 6.4, with operations that conflict when their accesses overlap and one is a write. Its edges come from declared sets only. A Merge landing also depends on the head at the paths it merges, and no edge records that dependence; Theorem 5.1 takes it into account through patch compatibility instead. The Lean definition indexes entries by list position, so versions \(u\) and \(v\) appear as positions \(i=u-1\) and \(j=v-1\): the write-read condition \(u\leq b_v\) is \(i+1\leq b_v\), written i + 1 \(\leq\) ej.base, and the read-write condition \(b_u<v\) is \(b_u\leq j\), written ei.base \(\leq\) j.

3 Single landings

The results of this section are Part I’s results about one landing at a time, in the adapted forms the model supports. Each is used in the batch theorem.

Lemma 3.1 (Frame; Part I, Lemma 6.3). Let \(b\leq v\). If \(X\not\bowtie\mathsf{W}_u\) for every entry \(e_u\) with \(b<u\leq v\), then \(S_{b}\equiv_XS_{v}\).

LSTBLOCK17X

Proof. A landing changes the head only within the change’s write set: where \(f(B)(p)=B(p)\), the merge returns the head’s value, and the frame condition gives \(f(B)(p)=B(p)\) for \(p\notin\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\). By induction on \(v\), a path that no \(\mathsf{W}_u\) with \(b<u\leq v\) covers keeps its value from \(b\) to \(v\). Now let \(p\in\mathrm{reg}(X)\) and suppose some \(\mathsf{W}_u\) covers \(p\), with \(x\bowtie p\) for \(x\in X\) and \(w\sqsubseteq p\) for \(w\in\mathsf{W}_u\). If \(x\sqsubseteq p\), then \(x\) and \(w\) are both ancestors of \(p\) and are comparable; if \(p\sqsubseteq x\), then \(w\sqsubseteq x\). Either way \(X\bowtie\mathsf{W}_u\), against the hypothesis, so \(p\) is covered by no later write set and keeps its value. ◻

Theorem 3.2 (Landing outcomes; Part I, Theorem 6.4, parts 1 to 3). Let entry \(e_{n+1}=(c,b)\) have \(b\leq n\). If its outcome is not \(\mathrm{Conflict}\), then \(S_{b}\equiv_{\mathsf{R}}S_{n}\). If its outcome is \(\mathrm{FastForward}\), then \(b=n\) and \(S_{n+1}=f(S_{n})\). If its outcome is \(\mathrm{Rebase}\), then \(S_{n+1}(p)=f(S_{b})(p)\) for \(p\in\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\) and \(S_{n+1}(p)=S_{n}(p)\) otherwise.

LSTBLOCK18X

Proof. An outcome other than \(\mathrm{Conflict}\) means \(\mathsf{R}\) meets no set in \(\mathrm{Later}(b,n)\), and Lemma 3.1 with \(X=\mathsf{R}\) gives the first claim. \(\mathrm{FastForward}\) means \(\mathrm{Later}(b,n)\) is empty; its length is \(n-b\), so \(b=n\), and a landing on its own base installs the change, because the merge with \(O=B\) returns \(T\). \(\mathrm{Rebase}\) means \(\mathsf{R}\cup\mathsf{W}\) meets no later write set, so \(S_{b}\equiv_\mathsf{W}S_{n}\) by the frame lemma. On \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\) the head therefore equals the base and the merge takes the change. Off \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\) the change equals the base and the merge takes the head. ◻

Part 2 differs from Part I’s statement, which concludes \(S_{v-1}(r)=\pi_1(c)\): commits do not appear in the model, so the statement is about versions and states instead. FastForward is defined, as in Part I, by the absence of later entries; once the base is capped, that is equivalent to a base equal to the head, which is the form Part I’s proof uses.

Theorem 3.3 (Serializability relative to declared reads; Part I, Theorem 6.4). Let \(\mathcal{L}\) be clean. Then for every \(v\), \(S_{v}\) equals the serial execution \(f_v(\cdots f_1(s_0)\cdots)\) of the first \(v\) transactions in log order; and for every entry \(e_v\), \(S_{b_v}\) agrees on \(\mathsf{R}_v\) with the serial execution of the first \(v-1\) transactions.

LSTBLOCK19X

Proof. The step is a lemma: if \(B\equiv_{\mathsf{R}\cup\mathsf{W}}H\), then \(\mathrm{land}(B,H,f(B))=f(H)\). On \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\), \(H(p)=B(p)\), so the merge takes \(f(B)(p)\), which equals \(f(H)(p)\) by the reads condition. Off \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\), \(f(B)(p)=B(p)\), so the merge takes \(H(p)\), which equals \(f(H)(p)\) by the frame condition. A clean outcome means \(\mathsf{R}_v\cup\mathsf{W}_v\) meets no later write set, and the frame lemma gives \(S_{b_v}\equiv_{\mathsf{R}_v\cup\mathsf{W}_v}S_{v-1}\). Induction on \(v\) then gives the first claim. For the second, the frame lemma with \(X=\mathsf{R}_v\) gives \(S_{b_v}\equiv_{\mathsf{R}_v}S_{v-1}\), and \(S_{v-1}\) is the serial state. ◻

The Lean proof goes through a weaker hypothesis than cleanness: every entry’s \(\mathsf{R}\cup\mathsf{W}\) meets no write set landed after its base. Validation is one way to obtain it; Section 6 uses another. The theorem is a state equality. Part I proves conflict-serializability by the serializability theorem of Bernstein, Hadzilacos and Goodman [5, Theorem 2.1]; the model proves the semantic statement directly and proves separately the direction of that theorem the argument uses (Proposition 3.7).

Proposition 3.4 (Merged writes; Part I, Proposition 6.5, at file level). For any entry \(e_{n+1}=(c,b)\) with \(b\leq n\), write \(B=S_{b}\), \(H=S_{n}\) and \(C=f(B)\). Then \(S_{n+1}(p)=H(p)\) for \(p\notin\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\); \(S_{n+1}(p)=C(p)\) for \(p\in\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\) that no set in \(\mathrm{Later}(b,n)\) covers; and the merge of \((B,H,C)\) at every path that is not a conflict path is the value \(S_{n+1}(p)\).

LSTBLOCK20X

Proof. The first claim is the confinement of a landing to its write set. For the second, the pointwise frame argument gives \(H(p)=B(p)\), so the merge takes \(C(p)\). For the third, the merge at a non-conflict path is a value, and the landing installs that value. ◻

The proposition holds for every landing, not only for those validated as Merge. At file granularity it is the whole of Part I’s Proposition 6.5. The case that Part I’s proposition exists for, two edits to different lines of one shared file, is Theorem 4.2.

Theorem 3.5 (Independence; Part I, Theorem 6.7). Changes \(c_i,c_j\) satisfy \(\mathsf{W}_i\not\bowtie(\mathsf{R}_j\cup\mathsf{W}_j)\) and \(\mathsf{W}_j\not\bowtie(\mathsf{R}_i\cup\mathsf{W}_i)\) if and only if \(\mathrm{val}((\mathsf{W}_i),\mathsf{R}_j,\mathsf{W}_j)=\mathrm{Rebase}\) and \(\mathrm{val}((\mathsf{W}_j),\mathsf{R}_i,\mathsf{W}_i)=\mathrm{Rebase}\). 4 Merge resolution

4.1 Laws of the three-way merge

Proposition 4.1 (Merge laws). For states \(B,O,T\):

  1. a path is a conflict path if and only if the merge reports a conflict there;

  2. if no path is a conflict path, \(\mathrm{M}(B,O,T)\) equals \(O\) where \(O\) differs from \(B\), equals \(T\) where \(T\) differs from \(B\), and equals \(B\) elsewhere;

  3. any state with the property of part 2 equals \(\mathrm{M}(B,O,T)\);

  4. the merge of \((B,O,T)\) equals the merge of \((B,T,O)\), conflicts included;

  5. merging \((B,B,T)\) gives \(T\), merging \((B,O,B)\) gives \(O\), and merging \((B,X,X)\) gives \(X\);

  6. if \(O\) changes \(B\) only within \(\mathsf{W}_o\), \(T\) changes \(B\) only within \(\mathsf{W}_t\), and \(\mathsf{W}_o\not\bowtie\mathsf{W}_t\), then no path is a conflict path.

LSTBLOCK25X

Proof. Parts 1, 2, 4 and 5 are case analyses on the three equalities among \(B(p)\), \(O(p)\) and \(T(p)\) at each path. For part 3, a state with the property cannot exist when some path is a conflict path, since it would equal both \(O(p)\) and \(T(p)\) there; so no hypothesis is needed, and elsewhere the property fixes every value. For part 6, a conflict path differs from \(B\) on both sides, so it is covered by both \(\mathsf{W}_o\) and \(\mathsf{W}_t\), and two sets that cover one path meet. ◻

Uniqueness is the sense in which Part I’s description of the merged state determines it. Commutativity and idempotence hold here because a location is merged as a single value. They do not hold for positional diff3 in general: Khanna, Kunal and Pierce show that idempotence and stability fail, and that the obvious formulations of the intuition that edits to well-separated regions never conflict are wrong [8]. The laws are therefore laws of the model’s merge, and they transfer to an implementation only as far as its merge agrees with the model’s on the inputs it sees.

4.2 Line-level refinement

Theorem 4.2 (Line-level refinement). Let \(f\) be a file path, and suppose that for every line key \(\ell\), \(O(f\cdot\ell)=B(f\cdot\ell)\) or \(T(f\cdot\ell)=B(f\cdot\ell)\). Then for every \(\ell\), the path \(f\cdot\ell\) is not a conflict path, the merge there is \(\delta(B,T,O)(f\cdot\ell)\), and \(\delta(B,T,O)(f\cdot\ell)=\delta(B,O,T)(f\cdot\ell)\).

LSTBLOCK26X

Proof. At each line one side equals the base, so the line is not a conflict path. If \(O\) equals the base there, the merge takes \(T\), which is also what \(\delta(B,T,O)\) and \(\delta(B,O,T)\) give. If \(T\) equals the base, all three give \(O\). ◻

No hypothesis excludes a file-granular conflict, and Section 7 gives a case with both: the whole-file contents differ on both sides, but each line changed on at most one side. A pair of edits declared at file granularity is then validated as Merge, and treating the file as a single location would report a conflict. With lines as child locations, the merge is clean and keeps both edits, which is Part I’s Proposition 6.5 in the case that motivates it. The theorem requires stable keys, so it says nothing about insertions that shift later lines.

4.3 Patch compatibility

Theorem 4.3 (Merged landing as a serial step). Let \(m=(c,B)\) be a member and \(H\) a state such that no path is a conflict path of \((B,H,f(B))\). Then \(\mathrm{M}(B,H,f(B))=f(H)\) if and only if \(\mathrm{PC}(m,H)\).

LSTBLOCK27X

Proof. By Lemma 2.10, \(\mathrm{M}(B,H,f(B))=\delta(B,f(B),H)\). Off \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\), the frame condition gives \(f(B)(p)=B(p)\), so the diff returns \(H(p)\), and it gives \(f(H)(p)=H(p)\); the two sides agree for every transaction. On \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\), equality of the two sides is the definition of \(\mathrm{PC}(m,H)\). ◻

The theorem makes patch compatibility exact for a single member: a conflict-free merged landing is a serial step of its transaction precisely when the condition holds. Members validated as FastForward or Rebase never need it, and for changesets it has a direct reading.

Proposition 4.4 (Clean members and changesets). If \(B\equiv_{\mathsf{R}\cup\mathsf{W}}H\), then \(\mathrm{PC}((c,B),H)\). For a changeset, \(\mathrm{PC}((\chi(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{W},\Phi),B),H)\) holds if and only if \(H(p)=B(p)\) for every \(p\in\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\) with \(\Phi(p)=B(p)\).

LSTBLOCK28X

Proof. For the first claim, take \(p\in\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\). Agreement gives \(H(p)=B(p)\), and the reads condition gives \(f(H)(p)=f(B)(p)\). The diff returns \(f(B)(p)\) when the change moved \(p\), and \(H(p)=B(p)=f(B)(p)\) when it did not. For the second, the changeset gives \(f(H)(p)=\Phi(p)\) on \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\), and the diff gives \(\Phi(p)\) where \(\Phi(p)\neq B(p)\) and \(H(p)\) where \(\Phi(p)=B(p)\). The two agree exactly when \(\Phi(p)=B(p)\) implies \(H(p)=\Phi(p)=B(p)\). ◻

A changeset is patch-compatible when it never writes a stale base value back over a path the head moved. A changeset carries content for every path of its write set. Where that content equals the base, the change did not touch the path: the merge then keeps whatever the head holds, while running the changeset writes the base value back. The condition says these two readings never diverge. A direct consequence follows from the second claim, by a one-line argument outside Lean: if every path covered by \(\mathsf{W}\) receives content different from its base content, then every conflict-free merged changeset is patch-compatible. Part I’s touched paths contain the changed paths (Part I, Definition 4.2), but equality of the two sets is not established there, so we do not claim the consequence for the implementation.

Proof. Let \(x=y=0\) initially. The entry \(c_x\) sets \(x\) to \(1\) as a fast-forward, so the head has \(x=1\) and \(y=0\). The example matters because it defeats the two conditions one would try first. One might expect a blind write to be safe, since it reads nothing that could be stale, and one might expect a merged path outside the read set to be safe, since no read of it can be invalidated. The member \(m\) is both, and its merged landing is still not the serial execution of \(m\) on the head. The landed state is arguably the one the writer wanted: it keeps \(c_x\)’s update to \(x\), which \(m\) never meant to change, and no update is lost. But it is not the state of serial execution in landing order (\(c_x\), then \(m\)), because the transaction \(m\) says that \(x\) becomes \(0\). It is the state of the other serial order, \(m\) then \(c_x\): the merge places \(m\)’s stale write of \(x\) before \(c_x\)’s, as Thomas’s write rule does for obsolete writes [5, Section 4.5]. The theorem shows only that the merged landing differs from serial execution in ticket order; it does not show that no serial order explains it. A serializability statement in ticket order about merged landings must therefore either describe them as patches, as the serial merge of Definition 2.13 does, or assume patch compatibility.

5 Batch landing soundness

Theorem 5.1 (Batch landing soundness). Let \(\mathcal{L}\) be a log of \(n\) entries with no Conflict landing, let \(H=S_{n}\), and let \(e_1,\dots,e_k\) be an accepted batch at \(H\) with \(e_i=(c_i,b_i)\) and members \(m_i=(c_i,S_{b_i})\).   1. the batch state \(\mathrm{oct}(H;\mu_H(m_1),\dots,\mu_H(m_k))\) equals the serial execution \(f_k(\cdots f_1(H)\cdots)\) of the members in ticket order;

  2. the final snapshot of the log \(\mathcal{L}\) followed by \(e_1,\dots,e_k\) equals the batch state;

  3. the declared-access serialization graph of that log has no cycle.

LSTBLOCK30X

Proof. The proof has four steps, each a lemma of the development.

Disjoint writes and no conflict paths. The write half of the pairwise rule gives \(\mathsf{W}_i\not\bowtie\mathsf{W}_j\) for \(i\neq j\). Every accepted member has no conflict path against \(H\): a Merge member by acceptance, and a FastForward or Rebase member because its base and \(H\) agree on \(\mathsf{W}\) (Theorem 3.2), so the head never moved a path the change wrote.

The octopus merge is sequential landing. With pairwise disjoint write sets, the batch state equals landing the members one at a time in ticket order. The proof is by induction on the members, at each path \(p\). If the first member does not cover \(p\), its merged change leaves \(H(p)\), and every later member sees the same head value at \(p\). If it covers \(p\), no later member does, so their merged changes keep the head at \(p\), and both sides take the first member’s value.

Sequential landing is the serial merge. Each sequential landing is conflict-free, so it installs the three-way merge. At a path that a later member writes, the earlier members left the head unchanged, so a conflict path against the intermediate state would already be one against \(H\).

The serial merge is serial execution. By induction, with patch compatibility for every member: Merge members by hypothesis, and the others by Proposition 4.4. The first member’s merge equals \(f_1(H)\) by Theorem 4.3. 6 Under-declared reads

Part I’s guarantees hold relative to declared reads. A writer that reads a location without declaring it gets no protection for that read. This section makes precise what survives. The transaction’s own set \(\mathsf{R}\) now stands for its true read set \(\mathsf{R}^{*}\), and validation uses a separately declared set \(D_v\) for each entry, which may miss part of \(\mathsf{R}^{*}\).

Definition 6.1 (Declared reads and the write check). A log is validated with declared sets \(D\) when every entry has \(b_v\leq v-1\) and \(\mathrm{val}(\mathrm{Later}(b_v,v-1),D_v,\mathsf{W}_v)\) is \(\mathrm{FastForward}\) or \(\mathrm{Rebase}\). A log passes the write check when every entry has \(b_v\leq v-1\) and no conflict path of \((S_{b_v},S_{v-1},f_v(S_{b_v}))\). The undeclared part of a true read set is \(\mathsf{R}^{*}\setminus D\).

Validation with any declared sets implies the write check, because a clean outcome means that the head agrees with the base on the write set.

7 Non-vacuity

A theorem whose hypotheses can never hold is true and says nothing. The risk is real here. With a file modelled as one location, two different edits to one file always conflict, so a conflict-free Merge member could only be degenerate: identical changes on both sides, or declared sets that overlap while the changes touch different locations. An earlier version of the development had no constructed accepted batch at all, and the adversarial review of that version (Section 10) observed that the headline’s hypotheses were satisfiable only on paper. The witnesses below are closed Lean proofs that the hypotheses of the main positive theorems hold in nontrivial instances, with non-empty logs, bases below the head, and a Merge member that combines two edits to one file.

The line-level witness has a file \(F\) with lines \(F/1=10\) and \(F/2=20\), and a second file \(G\). Validated against \(c_0\), it is a Merge. The file-granular merge of \(F\) conflicts, since both sides changed \(F\) to different contents, but no path is a conflict path, each line is unchanged on one side, and the landing keeps both edits. 8 Findings from the formalization

Four statements changed when the model was written down in Lean. Each corrects or sharpens a statement of Part I.

8.1 The merge rule and idempotence

Part I’s proof of its Theorem 6.4 describes the three-way merge as taking, at each path, the side that differs from the base. The first formal definition followed that description: take theirs if ours equals the base, ours if theirs equals the base, and a conflict otherwise. Under that rule, merging two identical changes is a conflict at every path where they differ from the base, so the merge of \((B,X,X)\) is not \(X\) and idempotence fails. The corrected rule adds the clause of diff3 and Git: when both sides made the same change, the result is that change. With it, a path is a conflict exactly when both sides changed it to different values (Proposition 4.1, part 1), which is how diff3 and Git characterize a conflict. The failure under the first rule is a one-line computation and is not a Lean theorem; the development contains only the corrected rule. Part I’s results do not depend on the missing clause, because in the case its proof treats only one side differs from the base. The description in that proof should nevertheless read: the side that differs from the base, or the common value when both sides made the same change. Two agents that apply the same fix to one file would otherwise be told they conflict.

8.2 The frame lemma and its two regions

Part I states the frame lemma with agreement on \(\bigcup_{x\in X}\mathrm{sub}(x)\) and confines each landing’s changes to \(\bigcup_{w\in\mathsf{W}}\mathrm{sub}(w)\) (Part I, Lemma 6.3 and Assumption 6.2). The formalization shows that the two regions cannot both be the overlap region. Take \(X=\{a/b\}\) and \(\mathsf{W}=\{a/c\}\), which do not overlap. If a write with this \(\mathsf{W}\) were allowed to change any path in \(\mathrm{reg}(\mathsf{W})\), it could change the ancestor \(a\). But \(a\in\mathrm{reg}(X)\), so the snapshots before and after would disagree on \(X\) although \(X\) meets no later write set, and the lemma would be false. The model therefore takes agreement on \(\mathrm{reg}(X)\), which a model that gives content to every node needs, and confines writes to \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathsf{W})\). This is again a direct argument, not a Lean theorem; the Lean frame lemma is the corrected statement.

Part I’s lemma remains correct as stated, because its agreement excludes ancestors and its directories carry no content. The finding is that this choice carries weight. In Git, a directory has content of its own, the identifier of its tree, and that identifier changes whenever anything below it changes. A model that compared directory identifiers on reads, for instance to record that a writer listed a directory, could not keep the frame lemma with writes confined to the subtrees of the write set. Such a read must be declared as the directory prefix itself, whose subtree contains every write below it, as Part I’s access sets already do.

8.3 Merged landings and patch compatibility

Part I’s Proposition 7.4 excludes members validated as Merge, and its Proposition 6.5 describes the merged state without a serializability claim. The formalization extends the batch result to Merge members and shows what the extension costs. Two hypotheses suggest themselves: that the merged member is a blind write, so no read can be stale, and that its merged paths lie outside its read set. Proposition 4.5 satisfies both and is not serial in ticket order. The exact condition for one member is patch compatibility (Theorem 4.3), and under it the batch theorem covers Merge members (Theorem 5.1).

The correction to Part I concerns changesets. Part I’s Definition 8.1 appends a changeset validated as Merge with the effective change produced by the three-way merge, and its Proposition 8.3(3) states that, for FastForward and Rebase, the new branch state is the previous one with \(\Phi\) applied. Theorem 4.3 and Proposition 4.4 show that the effective change of a merged changeset equals \(\Phi\) on \(\mathsf{W}\) exactly when no written path whose content equals its base content was moved by the head. Statements about merged changesets that speak of applying \(\Phi\) need that condition.

8.4 The premise behind undeclared reads

The question of what survives under-declared reads was first posed as dropping Part I’s sound-refinement assumption (Part I, Assumption 6.2). That assumption concerns refined write sets: it requires each landing to change only locations that its refined writes cover. A writer that reads more than it declares violates a different premise, that declared reads cover true reads, which Part I uses implicitly in the qualifier “relative to declared reads” of its Theorem 6.4. Section 6 drops that premise and keeps Assumption 6.2. Without it, no landed update is lost, serializability can fail, and serializability holds when the undeclared reads meet no later write, a condition that cannot be weakened in general.

9 Limits of the formalization

The positional three-way merge is not modelled. Lines are child locations with stable keys, so an insertion that shifts later lines, a deletion, and the alignment of hunks are outside the model. Theorem 4.2 and the merge laws hold for this abstraction. Khanna, Kunal and Pierce show that the corresponding laws fail for positional diff3 [8], so they cannot be transferred to it without further work.

Validation works at one granularity. Refined write sets \(\mathsf{W}^\rho\), which Part I’s stale-read test uses, and opaque entries, which Part I’s validation treats as conflicts, are not modelled; Part I’s Corollary 6.8 on symbol granularity is not formalized. The log has one ref, so entries on other refs, and Part I’s restriction of \(\mathrm{Later}\) to entries on the same ref, do not appear.

Agreement differs from Part I’s, as Section 2 describes. The Lean relation compares every path in the overlap region, including ancestors and directory nodes, where Part I compares only file and symbol locations in the subtrees. Content is assigned to each node independently, so a file whose content is determined by its symbols or lines is not represented: a write declared at symbol granularity changes the symbol node and not the file node. The mappings of Part I’s Lemma 6.3 and Theorem 6.4 to Lean theorems are therefore adapted, not literal, and the correspondence with Part I’s relation when ancestors carry no content is an informal observation.

The log is simpler than Part I’s in ways that remove failure cases rather than add guarantees. A conflicting landing falls back to the change’s value instead of being refused, so the log is total. Each change is computed on the snapshot at its base, so the consistent-base assumption cannot fail. And the log starts at version 0 rather than at an arbitrary version.

The ref and projection results take as hypotheses what Part I proves of the code or assumes of the hash. Part I’s append discipline (Part I, Lemma 5.3) is a hypothesis of the ref results, and so is the ordering of commit generations. In the Git projection, a commit is identified with the data it encodes, which models content addressing with no collision.

Several of Part I’s results are not formalized: the rewrite cost (Proposition 4.3), replay determinism and checkpoints (Theorem 5.1), tamper evidence (Proposition 5.2), properties 1 to 3 and 5 of combiner safety (Theorem 7.2), the symbol-granularity corollary (Corollary 6.8), repairs (Definition 7.5), and the correspondence with Kung and Robinson [9] as a theorem. The generic serializability theorem of Bernstein, Hadzilacos and Goodman [5] is not formalized either; the model proves the direction Part I uses and proves serializability as a state equality.

The code is not verified. The theorems are about the model. The implementation is connected to the model by the code-evidence appendices of Parts I and III, which rest on reading code at named commits. Part I reports that the implementation’s three-way merge agrees with git merge-file on a differential test, which is evidence about the merge and not a proof. The check that enforces the pairwise rule in the implementation is described in Part I (Part I, Section 7.3). Whether the implementation’s merged landings satisfy patch compatibility is not established by this paper or by any earlier Part.

10 Trusted computing base

The development is checked by Lean 4 at version 4.35.0, fixed in the project’s toolchain file, and uses core Lean only: no Mathlib and no package dependencies. It contains 180 theorem declarations, helpers included. No proof uses sorry or admit, no axiom is declared, and no definition is unsafe or partial.

A reader must trust three things: the Lean kernel, the axioms the proofs use, and the claim that the definitions of Section 2 mean what the prose says. The kernel rechecks every proof term, including those produced by tactics and by evaluation of decidable propositions, so tactics are outside the trusted base. The third item cannot be checked by Lean, which is why this paper shows the core definitions as listings. A few helpers appear in the listings without one: Outcome.clean holds for FastForward and Rebase, Coarsens and CoarsensAll say that every access has an ancestor in the coarser set, WritesDisjoint says that members’ write sets are pairwise disjoint, and Txn.write1, Txn.assign and Txn.patch build transactions that set one location, or a set of locations below the declared write set, from a function of the declared reads.

The first round asked whether each main theorem’s hypotheses could hold, whether the definitions model Part I faithfully, and whether the counterexamples exhibit what they claim. 12 Related work

The serializability arguments are classical. Kung and Robinson’s serial validation is the landing step [9]. The serialization graph and the theorem that an acyclic graph gives serializability come from Bernstein, Hadzilacos and Goodman [5]. The batch anomaly is phenomenon G2-item in Adya’s classification [1], with the shape of write skew in the terms of Berenson and colleagues [4], and Fekete and colleagues characterize when histories under snapshot isolation fail to be serializable [7]. The present development instantiates these arguments for one model and checks them mechanically, with the state equality of serial execution as the conclusion rather than graph acyclicity alone.

The write-write case is where source control differs from a database, and the three-way merge is its mechanism. Khanna, Kunal and Pierce give a formal investigation of diff3 and show that several properties one would expect of it fail in general [8]. The model here sidesteps their difficulties by merging per location and treating lines as locations with stable keys. It is therefore a statement about an idealized merge, and its laws should be read alongside their negative results. The transaction log itself follows the lakehouse formats, of which Delta Lake is the model for this series [3]. Lean 4 is described by de Moura and Ullrich [6].

The batch results have close precedents in deterministic and batched databases. Aria validates a batch of transactions against a snapshot and commits a transaction only if it has no write-after-write or read-after-write dependency on an earlier transaction in the batch; its serializability theorem gives serial execution in input order [15]. That rule is the pairwise rule of this paper, and for fast-forward and rebase members the batch result here is the same idea, machine-checked in a source-control model. Ding, Kot and Gehrke batch optimistic transactions and reorder them using an intra-batch dependency graph [16], and they also discuss the write skew that per-member validation admits. What these systems do not have is a three-way merge: the extension to merged members, and the patch-compatibility condition it needs, has no counterpart there that we found. The merge counterexample is an instance of Thomas’s write rule, under which an obsolete write is ignored and the result corresponds to a different serial order [5, Section 4.5].

On the merge side, Swierstra and Löh give a formal semantics of version control in which lines carry unique labels and independent patches merge, with a Coq model [17]. The line-level refinement here is the same abstraction. Sousa, Dillig and Lahiri verify three-way program merges against semantic conflict freedom, a property stated in terms of re-execution [18]. Mechanized proofs of isolation and transaction libraries exist for databases, for example VerIso for isolation guarantees [19] and vMVCC for a multi-version transaction library [20]. A search of these and related sources (30 sources, more than 40 queries) found no mechanized serializability proof for a Git-like source-control model with merges. Some bibliographic search services were unavailable during the search, so this is an absence of evidence, not a claim of priority.

13 Conclusion

The core of the transactional model of source control is machine-checked. In the model, a batch whose members are fast-forwards, rebases or conflict-free merges lands as the serial execution of its members in ticket order, provided no member reads or writes what an earlier member wrote and every merged member is patch-compatible with the head. Each hypothesis is necessary: write skew defeats the first, and a blind write that never read its merged path defeats the second. Patch compatibility is the exact condition for a merged landing to be a serial step, and for changesets it says that a change never writes a stale base value back over a path the head moved. Writing the model in Lean also corrected the merge rule, located the frame lemma’s dependence on its two regions, and identified the premise that undeclared reads violate.

Three gaps remain between these theorems and the system. The merge is an idealization with stable line keys, not positional diff3. The implementation is linked to the model by reading code, not by proof. And whether the implementation’s merged landings are patch-compatible is unknown; checking the condition at landing, or restricting changesets to paths whose content they change, would close that gap for changesets. Within the model, the foundation that the series’ thesis needs is now proved by machine: transactions with declared read and write sets, landed in batches by one serialized authority, are serializable under conditions that can be checked at landing.
